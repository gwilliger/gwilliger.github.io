3) SUNSET/SUNRISE PHOTOS (+2%): Take a series of photos of sunset from the same spot with the same field of view over the
semester.  I'd like at least six photos with around 10-14 days between each photo. 
You can take extra photos.  The point will be to record the change of
the Sun's position over time, so you MUST take the photo from the same
place, covering the same angle and view, each time. 
Record the date to within 5 minutes, time and position of each photo.
Both digital and film photos are allowed.
You will need about a 45-70 degree field of view (30-50mm lens on 35mm camera).
Fill in the Sunset/sunrise cover sheet and hand it in with your labelled photos.

**THE FOLLOWING IS IMPORTANT TO NOTE DEPENDING ON WHICH SEMESTER IT IS!**
AUTUMN SEMESTER: Put the setting sun toward the RIGHT side of your first photo.  It will move to the left.
Put the rising sun toward the LEFT side of your first photo.  It will move to the right.

SPRING SEMESTER: Put the setting sun toward the LEFT side of your first photo.  It will move to the right.
Put the rising sun toward the RIGHT side of your first photo.  It will move to the left.

A slightly wide angle lens,  say 35-40 mm, should work best. Give the location information in
sufficient detail so that someone can duplicate your photo. That's a scientifically useful photo.

Try to get enough of the Sun's disk so that it's obvious exactly where sunset is.  If the Sun is not exactly
on the horizon, it's OK.   As long as it's within a few solar diameters of the
horizon (about 2-3 degrees, since the Sun is 0.5 degrees across) a picture will
be OK.  If you'd rather take sunrise photos, you can.  Just be consistent. In that
case, start the Sun on the other side of your photo compared to the sunset version (WATCH
WHICH SIDE DEPENDING ON THE SEMESTER!), since
it will move in the opposite direction.

Each of the four photos MUST HAVE this supporting information to count for credit:
1) date and time (to the nearest 5 minutes)
2) observing location (close enough so someone can find it)
3) observing conditions (clear, cloudy, hazy, partly cloudy etc.)
4) Moon's right ascension and declination
5) Moon's ALTITUDE.  This is from 0 to 90 degrees, with 0 on the horizon and 90 at the zenith.
Halfway up is 45 degrees.  One quarter way up is 22.5 deg etc.  Your fist at arm's length is
about 10 degrees.  Give the altitude to the nearest 10 degrees.
6) Moon's AZIMUTH.  This is from 0 to 359 degrees.  0=north, 90=east, 180=south, 270=west.
Give it to the nearest 45 degrees (cardinal points N,NE,E,SE,S,SW,W,NW).  Give either a number or a
cardinal point.

 5) SUNSPOT PHOTOS (+2%):
This is ONLY available if the solar telescope is available at the planetarium.
(a) Take two photos of the sun with at least one sunspot using the solar telescope in the planetarium courtyard, about
4-10 days apart.  Note the day and time of each photo. The same sunspot should be visible in both photos. 
(b) Immediately after taking your photo, get a screenshot or download a photo of the sun from
www.spaceweather.com ("Daily Sun"), or
National Solar Observatory (NSO) images
or solarmonitor.org (which gives images from the NASA Solar Dynamics Observatory satellite). 
You should be able to make a clear connection between your
photo and the spaceweather.com or NSO images.  Both websites have archives if you need to dig back for an old image.
Use individual sunspot numbers for unambiguous references if at all possible.
(c) Using your photos and the online photos, choose a sunspot in both photos and estimate its latitude to the nearest
15 degrees (or better): 0, 15, 30, 45.  (You probably won't ever get a sunspot at >45 degrees.)
(d) Then for each photo, estimate the "longitude".  For our class, find north from the online images.  Orient your
photo north up.  Then east is right and west is left, and we use:
      center of disk:  0 longitude
      on eastern limb (edge), or all the way to the right if north is up: 90 deg E
      on western limb (edge), or all the way to the left if north is up: 90 deg W
(e) Estimate how far the sunspot went across the solar disk, in degrees of longitude.  Remember that you're looking at a sphere!
It might help to use a globe, imagine your sunspot on it and stare at it with one eye to determine the longitude difference.
(f) Print out your photos and the online photos on a standard printer.  Black and white is fine.  Label the sunspot you select.
(g) Write up your observations for each of your two observations on this form

Then write your difference in longitude.  Using the time between observations, estimate the rotation period of the sun
at the latitude of the sunspot.
Turn in your assignment by the last day of class, on paper.

Grading is 1/3 on your photos + the photos downloaded from spaceweather.com (or similar), 1/3 on your measurements and 1/3 on your calculations.
It's good for up to +2% on your participation grade.
